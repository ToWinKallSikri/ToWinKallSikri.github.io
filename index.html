Dissertation

A Quantitative CIE-Informed Change Management Framework for OT Attack Surface Reduction in CNI

MSc Cyber Security · Lancaster University · Supervised by Prof. Paul Smith

The problem. OT systems in Critical National Infrastructure are being digitalised, and every change (a new networked sensor, a remote-access path, a cloud service) can quietly erode the engineered protections around the physical process. ISO/IEC 27001, IEC 62443-2-1, NIST SP 800-82r3 and the Cyber-Informed Engineering (CIE) Implementation Guide all require change control, but none defines how to score the security impact of a single change.

What I built. The CIE-Informed Change Impact Metric (CCIM): a decision-support framework that turns CIE principles into measurable variables, scores a proposed OT change, and either approves it or sends it back for a CIE-guided redesign, while making the trade-off between security, usability and cost explicit.

Mar 2026 - Aug 2026
How CCIM works
  1. Describe the proposed OT change (e.g. a WAN-routed feedback loop, a new interlock, a cloud-hosted controller).
  2. Score four CIE-derived risk vectors: consequence exposure, engineered controls, digitalisation and interdependency.
  3. Weight them with AHP to reflect the organisation's risk appetite, then combine into an Attack Surface Delta Score.
  4. Divide by usability and cost-benefit scores to get a Trade-off Index.
  5. Compare with a calibrated threshold: pass, or escalate to a CIE-guided redesign.
22

OT change scenarios extracted from documented incidents (Stuxnet, Triton, Ukraine 2015, Oldsmar, Colonial Pipeline) and published mitigations (data diodes, SIS/BPCS independence, DMZ segmentation) to build the calibration set.

20,000

PERT-based Monte Carlo draws in Python (NumPy, fixed seed for reproducibility) used to simulate the score distribution for acceptable and unacceptable changes.

AUC ≈ 0.993

ROC analysis with Youden's J set the approval threshold (τ ≈ 0.277 for the worked weight profile), separating simulated incidents from mitigations almost perfectly.

Case studies and findings
  • Microgrid, WAN-routed PMU feedback: failed under both a nuclear and a manufacturing risk profile and was escalated for redesign. The physical-and-digital interlock mitigation passed comfortably under both.
  • AI-enabled chlorine dosing at a water treatment facility: a cloud-hosted optimiser only just passed (about 5% below the threshold), and a one-point change in a single score flipped the verdict. CCIM made that fragility visible where a compliance review against IEC 61508 could not.
  • Legacy vs CCIM: compared against standards-led change review, CCIM keeps the engineering content of the standards but adds a reproducible, auditable decision boundary.
  • Honest limits and next steps: scores and AHP weights are illustrative and need structured expert validation (inter-rater agreement), a larger calibration corpus, and a non-compensatory gate for extreme scores.

Built under a "minimum machine resources" brief: independent state machines communicating through global flags, all timing driven by a 1 ms Timer1 CTC interrupt, short ISRs to respect real-time constraints, and direct register I/O (DDRD, PORTD, PIND) with no Arduino functions or external libraries.
